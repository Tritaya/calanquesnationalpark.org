Kayak & SUP: the only craft that may land

No commercial boat may land passengers anywhere in this park — a paddler may. That single fact makes the kayak the most powerful vehicle in the Calanques, and since May 2023 the most regulated: who you rent from now decides where you may land, and the park's own English page doesn't mention it. Here is the licence system, the landing map, the ten legal launch points and the one wind rule that keeps people out of rescue boats.

TL;DR

  • Kayaks land where boats can't. Boat tours may never land in the park; a paddler may come ashore almost anywhere — the exceptions are a short, learnable list (Riou, Sugiton in quota season, the En-Vau/Port-Pin haul-out rule, Port-Miou's harbour, and every red day).
  • No permit, but a licence system. Paddling needs no ticket. Renting does: since 1 May 2023 only 8 park-approved operators may rent you a craft — and only 3 of them (all in Cassis) carry En-Vau/Port-Pin access rights in summer. Guided trips are exempt from all of it.
  • The park's wind rule is worth every safety brochure combined: "Je fais l'aller face au vent" — paddle out against the wind, come home with it. SUP here is a flat-morning activity, full stop.
  • Reading this before an En-Vau day? The short version: rent from CSLN, Lo Kayak or Corton Beach in Cassis — or take a guided kayak trip and skip the compliance question entirely.

The licence system, plainly (since 1 May 2023)

You cannot rent an unguided kayak or paddleboard for use in the park core from just anyone. Since 1 May 2023 the park approves (agrée) rental operators, and its official PDF lists exactly eight companies — with fleet sizes, which double as an honest census of how crowded the water gets:

Three rows matter more than the rest: only CSLN, Lo Kayak and Corton Beach — all Cassis-based — hold the additional authorisation to access the calanques of En-Vau and Port-Pin from 1 May to 30 September. The authorisation term runs 2023–2028. Renting from any of the other five is perfectly legal; it just doesn't carry En-Vau access rights in that window. And the widely-repeated claim that renting from the Marseille firms "puts you outside the regulation" is false — all eight are fully approved; what the non-Cassis five lack is one sector's summer pass.

The exemption that simplifies everything: guided trips. The park is explicit that the regime applies only to rentals — any outing accompanied by a qualified paddle-sport professional is exempt from all of it. That's why Raskas Kayak, Marseille's best-known sea-kayak outfit, isn't on the list: it's a guide, not a renter, and its absence is not a red flag. For a first-timer aiming at the En-Vau sector, the cleanest single purchase is a guided morning like the half-day sea-kayak tour from Cassis (★5.0, 117 reviews) — the guide carries the compliance question, the wind judgement and the route, and you carry a paddle.

The list is stamped 2023 — and it shows. The PDF the park still links in July 2026 is marked "mise à jour 2023", and two of the eight websites on it no longer exist: 123kayak.fr and kayak-attitude.fr don't resolve at all. Treat the list as authoritative on who is licensed and unreliable on how to reach them. Whether any operator has since been added or dropped isn't published anywhere — before booking an unguided rental for the En-Vau sector, confirm current status with the park on 04 20 10 50 00.

Bringing your own craft? The rental regime vanishes from your picture entirely. Every landing rule below still applies.

And if this all sounds like more law than a holiday paddle deserves: the Côte Bleue half-day kayak tour (★4.7, 944 reviews — the most-reviewed paddle product in the region) runs the limestone coast west of Marseille. To be clear about what it is: the Côte Bleue is not the national park — and that is exactly why renting, landing and picnicking there are simpler. Same blue water, same white rock, a fraction of the rulebook.

Where you may land (default: everywhere — then subtract)

First, why landing rights are the whole game. Swimming here is not incidental to the paddle — it is the product: oligotrophic water over white limestone and Posidonia meadow, routinely 10–20 m of visibility in settled weather, and you enter it from the craft, picking your spot in coves no shore visitor can reach. No pebble-hobbling, no towel-to-towel beach margin. One skill check before you commit: re-boarding a sit-on-top from deep water is the thing most first-timers have never done — practise it once beside the launch beach, not beside a cliff. (Where the water itself is best, cove by cove, is on the swimming & snorkeling page.)

Now the rules. The park's own printed paddle-sports sheet frames it the right way round: you may disembark freely throughout the park, except a short list. Here is the complete subtraction, from the park's kayak page, its February 2025 leaflet and its live regulation map:

The En-Vau rule, precisely — because every guide overstates it

Most coverage calls the summer regime at En-Vau and Port-Pin a "landing ban". The park's own GIS layers say otherwise, and they are the most precise statement available: the regulated act is tirage à terre — hauling the craft ashore — bounded by a surveyed offshore limit, with a cable (filin) provided for parking your boat. Decisively, En-Vau and Port-Pin are absent from the park's separate "landing prohibited" map layer, which contains only Riou and Pierres-Tombées.

There are exactly two filins in the whole park, both installed in 2022: one at En-Vau, one at Port-Pin. The Port-Pin cable runs along the eastern side of the inlet — on your right as you paddle in. Bring a short line for tying on. Two further kayak mooring buoys sit at La Ciotat: Figuerolles (balise 1B) and Mugel (balise 11B). The three official wordings (web page, printed sheet, GIS map) genuinely differ in breadth, so the operating rule to follow is the strictest useful one: between 1 May and 30 September, don't beach the boat at Port-Pin or En-Vau — moor to the cable — and check the park's live cartothèque for the current geometry.

One question the park has never answered in print: whether guided trips may haul out at En-Vau or Port-Pin in season. The guided exemption is written against the rental rule, not the haul-out rule. The park's authorisations desk (autorisations@calanques-parcnational.fr) is the arbiter — a guide will simply follow the filin rule, which is another argument for letting one decide.

The 10 legal launch points (and the park's own route planning)

"Je me mets à l'eau sur les sites autorisés" — the park instructs paddlers to enter the water only at authorised embarkation sites, and publishes exactly ten of them on its regulation map. Almost no travel guide reproduces the list:

Marseille side · 2 points

Callelongue — end of the southern coast road, slipway metres from the bus 20 terminus; the classic start for Cap Croisette, Île Maire and (long) Morgiou. Bains des Dames — on the Corniche, useful for Frioul and the near coast. Everything beyond Cap Croisette from here is committing open water.

Cassis side · 2 points

Grande Mer — the town beach, where CSLN and Lo Kayak actually put you on the water; showers, left-luggage and cafés minutes away. Port-Miou — quayside inside the inlet, 25–30 min on foot from town; the shortest water start for Port-Pin and En-Vau, realistic mainly with an inflatable or a trolley.

La Ciotat · 6 points

Nouveau port, Capucins, Cyrnos (two points), Lumière and Mugel — six of the ten legal launches are in one town, which tells you where the park is happiest to see paddlers. Add the two dedicated kayak moorings (Figuerolles, Mugel), a marked paddle channel in the town's buoyage plan and a fraction of the Cassis traffic: the least-regulated, quietest base on the coast, covered in our La Ciotat guide.

The park pairs the points with its own planning arithmetic, worth quoting because it calibrates everything else: from Pointe Rouge (Marseille), out-and-back to Cap Croisette is a half day and to the calanque de Marseilleveyre a full day. From Cassis, En-Vau and back is a half day (45 min–1 h each way; Port-Pin about 45 min) and the Devenson cliffs a full day — flat calm only. Callelongue to Sugiton is roughly 2 h each way; a circumnavigation of Île de Riou is about 21.5 km of open water, experienced paddlers with a guide only.

An honest gap in the official layer: Pointe Rouge and la Maronaise — the two most-used Marseille paddle bases, and Pointe Rouge is named in the park's own suggested itineraries — do not appear in the authorised-embarkation layer. The likely explanation is that both sit outside the park core, making them unregulated rather than prohibited, but the park hasn't confirmed that in anything it publishes. Call 04 20 10 50 00 before building a plan on it.

How far out you may go: the 300-metre rule

A rental kayak or SUP is legally an engin de plage — an unregistered craft — and may navigate anywhere in the park up to 300 metres from the coast, in daylight only. Extensions to 2 or 6 nautical miles exist under division 240, but only for craft meeting every one of that regulation's equipment criteria, which no rental sit-on-top does. The park's English page compresses all this to "up to six miles off the coast" — wrong for the boat you'll actually be in. Trust the French page; this site exists partly because the English ones can't be trusted. Also banned regardless of distance: paddling through marked swim zones and harbour basins (40+ prohibited polygons on the park's paddle map), the Pomègues aquaculture zone, mooring to yellow buoys, and taking anything in the seven no-take zones.

The wind rule that outranks everything else you'll read

The limiting factor here is not fitness — it's wind and exposure. Between the calanques the shoreline is vertical limestone with no landing, so a paddler caught by rising wind halfway between Port-Pin and En-Vau has one option: keep paddling. The coves are often glassy while the headlands between them are rough, which flatters beginners into over-committing. Two different winds, two different failure modes:

Mistral (NW)

The Marseille-side killer

Offshore on much of this coast — which is precisely the danger: the launch beach looks flat while the wind quietly pushes you out and builds a steep sea at every headland. It cancels departures from Callelongue and the Pointe Rouge side, and makes the Cap Croisette channel and any Riou crossing serious. It also drives up fire risk — the indirect way it closes your landing options.

Levant (E/SE) + S/SW swell

The Cassis-side killer

Counterintuitive because it isn't the famous wind. It drives swell straight into the east-facing coves and makes entering and landing at Port-Pin and En-Vau dangerous even in mild wind, as the swell breaks at the cove mouths. An E/SE forecast should cancel a Cassis paddle on the most beautiful-looking morning.

The park's own safety sheet contains the single most useful sentence in this entire subject: "Je fais l'aller face au vent" — do the outbound leg into the wind, so the tired return leg runs downwind. Most first-timers do the opposite, riding an easy tailwind out and discovering the price at the turnaround. Check Windguru or Windy plus Météo-France marine the evening before and the morning of, and launch between 08:00 and 09:30 — the sea is calmest before the thermal breeze builds late morning, the Port-Miou–En-Vau corridor is empty before 10:00, and the calanque walls are lit (afternoon puts them in shadow).

SUP is a different sport here. A board catches wind the way a kayak doesn't, and there is nowhere to shelter — a moderate kayak day is an unmanageable SUP day. The licensed fleet is the tell: roughly 432 kayaks to about 76 boards across the eight operators. Treat SUP as a flat-calm-morning activity near the launch — which, done on the right morning under the Cap Canaille cliffs, is genuinely lovely: the Cassis SUP session (★4.9, 81 reviews) is exactly that product, guided, morning-slot, sheltered water. If the plan is En-Vau, take the kayak.

Red days: paddle yes, moor yes, swim yes — set foot, no

From 1 June to 30 September, tomorrow's fire-risk access decision is published around 17:00–18:00 each evening (the full system, decoded). On a red day the massif is closed to all land access including arrival by sea — but the sea itself is not a massif, so navigation stays legal. For a paddler the split is surgical, and Lo Kayak — an En-Vau-authorised operator — publishes it verbatim in English: "Although access by sea remains authorized, it is strictly forbidden to disembark on the beaches or on the rocks. The mooring of kayaks and swimming are nevertheless possible near the beaches at the bottom of creeks."

  • Paddle — yes, the rental bases generally still open.
  • Moor the kayak at the head of a calanque — yes.
  • Swim and snorkel from the craft near the beach — yes.
  • Set foot on beach or rock, anywhere in the park — no. The prefectural wording bans access "y compris par la mer"; fines start at €135 per person.

So a red day destroys a beach picnic but not a snorkelling paddle — you could reach En-Vau and be legally unable to get out of the boat, which is fine if swimming was the point and miserable if sunbathing was. The claim circulating online that "kayaks are exempt on red days" is flatly false: the exemption covers navigation, never landing. Book paddling early in your trip so a red evening still leaves days to re-run it, and keep a plan B that floats — a boat tour is a real red-day substitute for a walk; a kayak trip is only a substitute for a swim. Red is rarer than the drama suggests — the coastal massif has gone red 3 times so far in 2026 — but it is decided the evening before, which is why non-refundable morning bookings hurt.

Ages & families: the operator, not the park, decides

The park sets no age minimum for paddling — every limit is operator-set, and the two Cassis operators we could fully verify diverge enough to decide which one a family can use at all:

  • Lo Kayak: no child under 6 in the craft — on rentals and guided trips. Every participant must swim 25 m without a buoyancy aid; no pregnant women. The sweeteners are real: 20% off rentals carrying an under-12, 50% off guided trips for under-12s, and 15% off on presentation of a same-day train ticket to Cassis.
  • CSLN's guided "3 Calanques" (4 h): minimum age 15, max 12 people per qualified guide. This is the single most-missed constraint on the most-booked guided product in Cassis — a family with a 13-year-old cannot take it, however capable the teenager.
  • The La Ciotat and Corton Beach minimums appear lower, but we could not verify their 2026 terms (one site blocks automated access) — confirm directly before booking. Age rules here are simply not standardised.

Honest summary: this is a good family activity within about 45 minutes of the launch, and a poor one beyond it. Whoever paddles: the park's floor is 1.5 litres of water per person per half day (there is no refill, no toilet, no shade and no kiosk anywhere on the route — nothing at En-Vau or Port-Pin at all), plus a dry bag for the phone, a long-sleeved top, closed or reef shoes, and a mask — swimming where nobody can walk to is the entire point. Life jackets are compulsory and supplied. Leave the drone: overflight of the core below 1,000 m is banned.

The timing answer nobody expects: mid-September to mid-October

Four constraints all release within a fortnight of each other: the daily fire-closure regime ends 30 September, the En-Vau/Port-Pin haul-out restriction lapses the same day, the August crowds vanish — and both Cassis operators keep renting to 31 October on low-season rates, with the sea still around 22–25°C. That combination recurs at no other point in the year. Second best is May–early June: quiet water, low-season prices, colder sea, and note the haul-out rule is already in force from 1 May. Neither Cassis operator advertises any November–February season.

In July and August, pick your hour instead: the Port-Miou–Port-Pin–En-Vau string is the single most congested paddle corridor in the park between roughly 10:00 and 16:00 — the licensed Cassis fleets alone put 246 kayaks and 31 boards on this water, before private craft and guided groups — and the two filins are a finite resource, so arriving at En-Vau at midday in August means queueing for cable space with everyone who left Cassis at 09:00. Before 09:00 the corridor is close to empty and the water at its calmest. The La Ciotat sector is consistently quieter than Cassis for the same landscape; the Marseille side is quiet at any hour — because it is hard, not because it is undiscovered.

Rent, take a guide, or don't paddle at all

The decisive question is not cost — it is (a) where you want to land and (b) whether you can read the wind:

Rent unguided if…

You're a competent paddler, launching before 09:00 into a settled forecast, with an objective within ~45 minutes of the base — Port-Miou, Port-Pin, the Cassis cliffs, the La Ciotat coves. From about €30–45 for a half day. For En-Vau in the May–September window, your renter must be CSLN, Lo Kayak or Corton Beach.

Go guided if…

It's your first time on this coast, you have children, you're a nervous paddler or weak swimmer, your objective is the Marseille side (Riou, Morgiou, Cap Croisette — committing water where the guide's wind judgement is the product), or you simply can't confirm a renter's licence. Browse the guided kayak & SUP list — the exemption makes it the clean route.

Don't paddle if…

The massif is red and a beach was the point (you'd reach En-Vau legally unable to step out); E/SE wind or S/SW swell is forecast on the Cassis side; you want the cheapest look at the coves — that's a boat circuit; or you'd rather walk — Port-Pin is ~45 minutes on foot from Port-Miou, free, on the hiking routes.
