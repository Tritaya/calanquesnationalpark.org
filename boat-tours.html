Boat tours: what the booking pages don't say

A boat is the only way most visitors will ever see En-Vau, and the one calanques product that survives a red fire day. But every commercial boat here is a look-don't-land product, the biggest Marseille operator won't sell you a ticket more than 48 hours out, and the wind that cancels Cassis isn't the one you think. TL;DR

  • No boat lands anywhere, ever. Commercial passenger disembarkation is banned along the whole park coast, every day of the year — the Park Charter names Morgiou, Sormiou and Callelongue explicitly, and the Cassis operator says it in plain English: no drop-offs; to reach beaches like En-Vau you walk. There is no water taxi, and no authorisation to run one exists. Anything sold as "boat to the En-Vau beach" is either mis-sold or an open-water swim stop.
  • The two ports are not interchangeable. Cassis puts you at the white-cliff calanques in 10–15 minutes for €21; Marseille gives you 20 km of coast for €30–€45 and up to 3h15 — and only its longest circuit reaches En-Vau at all.
  • Booking logic is inverted. Cassis needs no booking (kiosk, same day). Marseille's biggest operator only opens reservations 24–48 hours ahead, offline — while marketplace platforms sell equivalent boats months out. Nothing about this coast rewards booking the kiosk circuits early; a lot rewards knowing which seller does what.
  • The boat is the red-day product. When fire risk closes every trail, navigation stays legal and the boats keep sailing.

Cassis: the kiosk circuits, decoded

One operator runs the whole Cassis port: the GIE des Bateliers Cassidains — 12 historic shipowning families, 15 boats from 12 to 75 seats, five of them diesel-electric hybrids, all flying the park's blue Esprit Parc national label. The ticket office is on the quai Saint Pierre, behind the square Gilbert Savon, open seven days a week subject to weather. The circuits nest — each longer one contains the previous route. Two things the fare table doesn't tell you. First: every one of the four standard circuits is commentary-only — no stop, no swim, no landing, at any price. Second: the widely republished "€19 Cassis boat trip" is a stale price; 2026 starts at €21. The booking system carries Adults / Children / Babies categories, so reduced fares exist, but the operator doesn't publish the child rates — ask at the kiosk.

The one swim product — and who it's really for

The "3 calanques + arrêt détente" (€40, adult and child alike, four departures daily at 09:15 / 12:00 / 14:00 / 16:00) runs on the 12-seat boats — a hard cap of 12 passengers. The swim happens in the open sea at anchor off the En-Vau sector: a ladder or a jump into deep water, no beach, no shallows, no lifeguard, nothing to stand on. The operator restricts it to declared swimmers, which is doing real work in that sentence. If you wouldn't happily swim 50 m in deep water away from a boat, this is not the easy add-on it looks like — the honest alternative for actually standing on a calanque beach is a kayak from Cassis or walking in.

Kiosk logistics that decide how the day feels

  • No booking needed. Buy at the kiosk on the day; online booking exists but you still pay at the ticket office 30 minutes before departure. Refunds only when weather cancels or modifies the trip — not if you're late. Card, cash and chèques-vacances accepted.
  • Avoid 14:30–16:00. The operator's own words: in summer the dock is too crowded then to move around or board; it recommends 10:30–11:00 instead. Take a morning departure — a walkable quay and a cooler deck.
  • No toilets on board any Cassis boat — the operator states it flatly. Free public toilets sit next to the ticket office; use them before a 2h20 circuit. No food sold aboard either; some crews carry water.
  • Bring headphones. Live commentary is French; two QR codes inside the boat unlock a free audio guide in English, Spanish, Portuguese, Japanese and German — through your own earbuds only.
  • Boarding is two steps down from the quay, no gangplank, crew assisting. Manual wheelchairs are accepted on the larger boats (max two per sailing, phone ahead); strollers and dogs (except French category 1–2 breeds) are fine.

Which circuit? For most people the 5-calanque at €25: it covers the postcard three plus the Castelviel–Devenson wall, the best cliff scenery on the coast, without the hour of open-water transit the 8- and 9-calanque runs add. With under-10s, sun-sensitivity or a weak stomach, take the €21 hour.

Marseille: two companies, two quays, one booking trap

Two distinct operators work the Vieux-Port, and people book the wrong quay constantly. Croisières Marseille Calanques (CMC) sells and boards at the north-east corner of the harbour — angle Quai du Port / Quai des Belges, facing the brasserie La Samaritaine, five minutes from the Vieux-Port metro. Icard Maritime is at 1 Quai Marcel Pagnol on the opposite (Rive Neuve) side, 10–15 minutes' walk around the harbour head. Marseille circuits run eastward: the 2–2h30 Essentiel covers Callelongue, the Riou archipelago, Sormiou, the Cosquer cape, Morgiou and Sugiton; only the 3h15 Intégrale continues to En-Vau, Port-Pin, Port-Miou and the bay of Cassis.

Icard — the finer-grained ladder

Icard's Intégrale (3h15, Marseille→Cassis) prices at €33 adult / €25 for 4–11 / €29 for 12–17 (same rate for students and French AAH disability-allowance holders) / under-4 free / €100 family (2+2). Its July–August swim circuit is €40 flat, its Château d'If run €11. A ticket is required on board from age 1, even for free children. Commentary is advertised in French, English, Spanish, Italian, Russian and Mandarin — the most multilingual boat on the coast.

The booking inversion nobody explains: CMC — the biggest Marseille operator — opens reservations only 24 to 48 hours before departure, weather-dependent, and states its circuits are not sold online. You cannot book CMC weeks ahead; it's a kiosk-or-phone decision (04 91 58 50 58) made 1–2 days out. Meanwhile third-party platforms sell boat experiences on this same water months in advance with free cancellation. Where a platform's promise and an operator's own rule disagree, the operator's rule decides what actually sails.

The refusals that surprise people at the gangway

  • Luggage: CMC bans suitcases and large backpacks on board outright, for safety. No aggregator mentions this. If you're between hotels or off a cruise ship, plan around it.
  • Under-10s on swim circuits: both CMC and Icard refuse children under 10 on their Baignade products, full stop — the swim is unsupervised, in deep open water, and all passengers must be strong swimmers. Families who built the day around "the boat where the kids swim" need the €21 Cassis hour instead.
  • Pregnancy: CMC refuses pregnant women of 7 months or more on every circuit, and pregnant women outright on the swim circuit (along with reduced-mobility passengers).
  • Minimum passengers: every departure at every operator is conditional on favourable weather, availability and an unpublished minimum head-count — a real cancellation cause in shoulder season.

A fourth port most guides omit: La Ciotat, ten minutes by train beyond Cassis, where Les Amis des Calanques run west beneath the 394 m Soubeyranes cliffs — the only way to feel Cap Canaille from below — for €18–€34 (under-12s €6 off). The catch: a minimum of 15 passengers to sail at all, which quietly cancels shoulder-season weekday departures.

Wind & cancellation logic: two ports, two enemies

"The mistral is blowing, so the boats won't run" is bad advice at Cassis — and good advice at Marseille. The two ports cancel on different winds, and knowing which is which rescues days.

Cassis

Cancelled by the E/SE and S/SW

The operator's published rule: excursions are cancelled for strong East/South-East or South/South-West winds with swell, and only modified or cancelled for a strong mistral. The Cassis coast sits in the mistral's lee — the NW wind blows offshore there and flattens the water under the cliffs, while an easterly swell hits the coves head-on. Day-of schedule and forecast go up on the operator's Facebook and Instagram; they point visitors at Windguru, Météo-France and the Cassis webcam.

Marseille

Cancelled by the mistral

The runs south past Cap Croisette and east toward Cassis cross open water with a long north-west fetch, so a strong mistral genuinely cancels or curtails Vieux-Port departures (and the Frioul ferries with them). A 40 km/h mistral can mean a flat, brilliant, half-empty day on a Cassis boat and a refund queue in Marseille — and a 20 km/h easterly with a metre of swell can do the reverse. Check the wind direction, not just the speed.

The seasonal shape works in your favour: by Météo-France normals, August is the calmest month of the year on this coast (February–April the windiest) — so cancellations are rarest exactly when most people visit. One caveat from the closures system: mistral days are often the days fire risk closes the trails, so the flattest water tends to sit under the most-closed massif. That is an argument for the boat, not against it.

The sequencing rule that beats any forecast: boats are weather-contingent, indoor attractions are not. Book the boat first, and keep the Cosquer cave replica or the Cassis wine cellars as the pivot for a blown-out day.

Marketplace experiences vs kiosk circuits: when €90 beats €29

The kiosk circuits are the best raw sightseeing value in the park — nothing on any platform shows you En-Vau's cliffs for €21. But they are one specific product: a seat on an open deck for one to three hours, commentary, no stop. The marketplace boat experiences and catamaran and sailing trips sell a different product — half or full days on the water, guaranteed swim stops, small groups, often food — and for three kinds of visitor the higher price is the honest buy:

  • You want the swimming guaranteed, not hoped for. The kiosk swim boats are one €40 Cassis product capped at 12 seats plus two July–August-only Marseille circuits that refuse under-10s. The most-booked swim cruise from Marseille (★4.7, 1,409 reviews, around $90) builds the day around anchored swim stops in the park's water — the right first-timer pick when "we swam in the Calanques" is the sentence you're buying.
  • You want to make a day of it. Against a 3h15 circuit with no toilet break and no food, the full-day classic-ketch cruise with buffet and wine (★4.8, 479 reviews, around $135) trades commentary for hours at anchor, lunch aboard and time in the water — a different day entirely from a seat on a vedette.
  • You're staying east of the park. From Bandol, Sanary or Toulon, getting to the Cassis kiosk means fighting the same summer traffic the boat lets you skip. The Bandol 13-calanque tour (★4.5, 554 reviews, 3h15 at sea, around $54) sails the whole Cassis–Marseille run without you ever crossing the Gineste.

Two honesty notes that apply to every platform listing. Marketplace boats obey exactly the same law as the kiosk ones: no landing, anywhere, ever — a "swim stop at En-Vau" means open water near En-Vau, not the beach. And bookable-months-ahead with free cancellation is itself the feature CMC cannot offer: for cruise passengers and fixed itineraries, the platform's calendar — not the boat — is what you're paying the margin for.

One check that costs nothing: legal tour boats in the park core fly an orange pennant with an orange hull sticker. The park's warning is aimed at you, not the operator — absence of the signage marks an illegal service and exposes the passenger to prosecution. Look before you board.

Renting your own boat: the green-vignette system

Skippering yourself is the one way to stop where you like, swim off the hull and stay two hours in an empty cove — and it is the most heavily regulated visitor activity in the park. The regulation is aimed at you as much as the operator.

  • The sticker decides your legality. Only professionals holding the park's green vignette may rent you a motorboat for the core — and the park warns, in terms, that the sticker's absence marks an illegal service and exposes the customer to prosecution. Ask to see the vignette before you pay; check the company against the park's published list (540 authorised vessels across 46 entries, 2025 update).
  • No licence? Cassis only. The French no-licence ceiling is 6 hp / 4.5 kW — perhaps 4–6 knots. From Cassis (three authorised firms, 78 boats, 7 of them electric) that puts Port-Miou, Port-Pin and the En-Vau mouth within easy reach. Marseille's no-licence fleets sit at L'Estaque, pointed at the Côte Bleue: a 6 hp boat cannot reach Sormiou and return. A "Calanques" no-licence listing from L'Estaque is selling you a different coastline.
  • Peer-to-peer is legal — and the burden is shared. A private owner may rent out one boat they own, at most 5 times per year, with a prior online declaration whose acknowledgement you must carry at sea; the owner may neither drive nor supply a skipper. Caught without the paperwork, you're ordered out of the core on the spot and the owner faces up to €100,000 and two years' imprisonment for unauthorised commercial activity in a national park. The park itself advises renting from an authorised professional instead.
  • Where you may not stop outranks where you may go. Anchoring, stopping and drifting are permanently banned inside En-Vau and Port-Pin — buoys and dynamic positioning included. The Sugiton inner cove and seven Frioul calanques are ZIEM zones, closed to motor craft even with the engine off. Anchoring on Posidonia seagrass is illegal for any vessel anywhere in the park (penalties cited to €150,000) — anchor on the light-coloured sand, retrieve vertically, and use the free Donia app to see the seabed. 5 knots inside the 300 m band, and never tie to a yellow buoy.
  • The free-anchoring era is ending. The park's mooring scheme is converting anchorages to compulsory buoy moorings — west Pomègues, Sormiou, Morgiou and the bay of La Ciotat are named — so re-check the current buoyage before planning a specific cove.
  • Money, roughly: no-licence boats from Cassis run around €150–260 a half day / €250–400 a day, fuel extra (€40–120), deposit €500–1,500 on a credit card — indicative figures only; this market re-prices constantly, so confirm with the base. Split four to six ways it beats any cruise; for a couple, the maths favours the €21–€33 circuits.

The legal shortcut past all of it — the vignette homework, the anchoring map, the wind judgement — is a skippered charter: a professional drives, you swim, and the boat goes everywhere a private one legally may. The private skippered sailing charter from Marseille (★5.0, 121 reviews, about $975 for the whole group) is that product — per head, for six people, it lands near the cost of a rented bareboat without the liability. Remember the one thing no format buys: a landing on a famous beach. The rules bind your rented boat and the 75-seat vedette alike — if standing on sand is the goal, it's the kayak or the trail.

The red-day rule: navigation legal, landing banned

From June to September, a red fire-risk day closes every trail, crag and calanque road in the rated massif — and changes nothing about a boat tour. The fire arêté regulates access to forest massifs; the sea isn't one. Navigation, anchoring and swimming from the boat all stay legal, which is why the operators keep sailing and why the boat is the one calanques plan that cannot be closure-cancelled.

The flip side is absolute: the closure covers arrival "y compris par la mer" — including by sea. Stepping onto a rock, beaching a tender or swimming ashore from an anchored boat is the same €135-per-person offence as walking past the barrier. On a red day you can be legally afloat twenty metres from a beach you are legally forbidden to touch. Since no commercial boat may land you on any day, the tour you booked loses precisely nothing — and the Frioul archipelago, which is not a rated massif, stays open as the day's other loophole.
