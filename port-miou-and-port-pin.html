Port-Miou & Port-Pin: the Cassis calanques you can actually walk to

One is a 1.4 km marina fjord so flat you can push a stroller along it — and so misunderstood that the park's own English page implies it never closes. The other is the swim it leads to: 45 official minutes away, graded moderate, with the only real pine shade of any beach calanque in this sector. Here is the pair, honestly — including what each one is bad at.

TL;DR

  • Port-Miou is the easiest calanque in the whole park — a flat 25–30 minute walk from Cassis, a bus stop 400 m away, no permit, no fee. It is also a 487-berth working marina with no real beach: come for the walk, the quarry history and the gateway, not for a swim.
  • Port-Pin is where the swim is: the park's official route is graded moderate — 2 km, 45 min, +45 m — against En-Vau's difficult 7.8 km, 3 h, +360 m. That one contrast settles most family routing arguments. Pine shade, walk-in beach, and it fills by late morning in summer.
  • Neither needs booking; both close on red fire days — including Port-Miou, whatever the park's English page implies. Check the massif colour the evening before on the closures page's system, and remember the paradox: the calmest water in the park often sits under the most-closed trail.

Port-Miou — the marina fjord (and what it is not)

Port-Miou is a 1.4 km flooded limestone gorge, in places only ~40 m wide, running straight into the edge of Cassis. The Romans reportedly called it Portus melior — the better port — and the geometry explains why: squeezed between Cap Cacau to the west and Cap Cable to the east, it is naturally sheltered from both the mistral and the east wind. Pope Gregory XI's fleet rode out a storm here in October 1376. The same geometry is why it is now a 487-post marina (99 of them for visiting boats) rather than a wild cove.

That marina status is the thing to internalise before you go. French beach guides state flatly that swimming is impossible where the boats are; the "beach" at the head is a gravel strip a few metres across, used for boat access. Port-Miou is a corridor, not a destination — every walker bound for Port-Pin and En-Vau, every kayak group and every guided hike funnels along it between 10:00 and 17:00 in summer. What it offers instead:

  • The easiest calanque access in the park, full stop. Flat tarmac then a broad quarry track; the only stroller-tolerable and wheelchair-plausible calanque approach anywhere in the massif. Bus 373 or the Cassis tourist train puts you within 400 m of it.
  • The Sentier du Petit Prince: the park's signature short walk — graded easy, a 1.7 km loop, 30 minutes, 40 m of ascent — with enamelled interpretation plaques, a chapel, and an esplanade view over Cap Canaille. It is named for aircraft wreckage found offshore; Saint-Exupéry's actual aircraft was located near Riou in 2000, not here.
  • Industrial history most visitors walk straight past. Cassis stone paved the port of ancient Massilia; from 1896 the Belgian industrialist Ernest Solvay's quarry removed 6–7 million tonnes and cut an 800 m gash into the western cliff before closing in 1981. The protest here on 13 March 1910 — about 2,000 people — counts among France's earliest environmental demonstrations. The Château de Port-Miou, built for the quarry, now houses the harbour office; the park opens the tower's top floor free of charge from April to the end of October, generally Wednesdays, Saturdays and Sundays.

The red-day trap — and the park's own page feeds it. Port-Miou closes with the massif on red fire-risk days, 1 June–30 September. It is inside the park core, not inside the Cassis built-up area. Yet the park's English Port-Miou page says the calanque "is open to visitors all year round", which English-language guides read as an exemption and repeat as "always open". The prefecture-sourced red-day breakdown lists exactly what stays open on the Cassis side — the town centre and the Arène, Corton, Bestouan and Grande Mer beaches — and Port-Miou is not among them. Same trap, same shape, as the Callelongue coastal path on the Marseille side. The full three-layer system is on the closures page.

The counterintuitive wind rule. The cliffs that shelter Port-Miou from the mistral are the reason it holds 487 boats — and the mistral is precisely what closes it, because red fire days are typically strong-mistral days. The wind the inlet blocks at sea level is the wind drying the massif above it. So the flattest, calmest-looking water in the park routinely sits under the trail most likely to be shut. Never infer "the water is calm, so the path must be open" — and note the mirror image: Cassis boat departures are cancelled not by the mistral (offshore here) but by easterly and south-easterly swell pushing into the bay.

Port-Pin — the family calanque, by the numbers

Port-Pin — "pine harbour", after the Aleppo pines ringing it — is the only easily walkable calanque near Cassis that combines a real, swimmable beach with genuine natural shade. The park's own page calls it une calanque victime de son succès and records more than 1,000 people on peak summer days against a stated comfort capacity of about 300. Both halves of that sentence are useful: it is genuinely worth the walk, and you need to time it.

The single most useful piece of routing data in the whole Cassis sector is the official grade gap between Port-Pin and its famous neighbour:

Port-Pin (from the Port-Miou entrance)MOYEN — moderate2 km45 min+45 m

If you have children, limited time, doubtful knees or an August afternoon, that table is the answer: Port-Pin is the right call and En-Vau is the trap. Independent trackers put the actual walking at 20–30 minutes one-way from the Port-Miou entrance; the park's 2 km / 45 min figure is the official sheet (whether it means one-way or return is not stated — budget it as generous one-way and you will not be surprised).

What the grade hides — and what it does not:

  • The route is easy until the last 100 m. A broad, near-flat quarry piste along Port-Miou (past the Pointe Cacau ore chutes), a low saddle, then the one hazard: a short final descent on polished, sharp limestone the park itself flags as roches glissantes et coupantes. It has injured people in sandals. Trainers with real grip are the minimum; this descent is the decision point for anyone with a stick or balance issues.
  • The shade is real and rare. The pine grove throws shadow over part of the beach and the rock slabs behind it — something En-Vau does not offer at all. In July–August the shaded spots are taken by 09:00–10:00 and the beach is effectively full by late morning; after 17:00 it thins as day-trippers leave for the last shuttles.
  • Family reality: fine from about age 6 with supervision on the descent; ages 3–5 get carried on the last stretch; babies only in a proper hiking carrier. Strollers reach a viewpoint, not the beach. Dogs are welcome on a lead (the Calanques admits leashed dogs in its core — unusual for a French national park).
  • The ruin at the back of the beach is a guinguette — an open-air café that ran until the 1970s–80s, when cars could still drive to the shore. The cove appears in the 1970 film Borsalino, and a German WWII coastal battery survives on the eastern cliff.

Best windows: before 09:30 any summer day (low sun over the eastern rim lighting the water, workable temperatures, shade still free), or September weekdays — the sea holds 22–25 °C, the crowds are gone, and the massif is usually open. Avoid 11:00–16:00 in July–August entirely.

Getting there — the €1.70 answer Verified: 30 July 2026

Everything below funnels through one trailhead: the park welcome panel at the Port-Miou entrance, end of Avenue des Calanques. There is no road, car park or boat landing at Port-Pin itself — ever.

  • The system that works: park free at the Gorguettes park-and-ride (signposted from the A50 exit, ~220 spaces, motorhome-friendly), then shuttle 373 via Bestouan to the "Calanques" and "Presqu'île" stops, ~400 m from Port-Miou. €0.90 a trip, €1.70 return, free under 6, paid on board by cash or card. Running 4 April–1 November 2026 on weekends, school holidays, public holidays and bridge days, and daily 09:00–20:00 in July–August. Line 372 serves the town centre on the same pattern (to midnight in July–August).
  • On foot from Cassis: about 30 minutes from the town centre to the park entrance per the National Park — the tourist office says "about 15", and the gap is simply where you start counting; budget 25–30 from the old port. Port to Bestouan to Avenue des Calanques, flat the whole way. Add 20–30 minutes more to Port-Pin.
  • Driving in anyway: the private Parking de la Presqu'île (~400 spaces, pine-shaded) is the closest and saturates by about 09:30 in July–August. Its tariff is genuinely unclear — commonly reported around €10/day, with older reports of €8 and of cash-only payment, and no official tariff page confirms a figure — treat the exact price as volatile and carry cash. Avenue des Calanques itself is residents-only.
  • From Marseille: TER train Saint-Charles–Cassis in ~25 minutes, but the station sits ~3 km uphill from town (local shuttle, then walk) — realistic door-to-trailhead 1h20–1h45. The regional coach from Castellane costs €3.50 single; its line number is in flux (the operator lists 78, older Cassis timetables say M8), so verify the number before travelling. Full options on the getting-there page.

Live disruption — check before planning a wide loop. Construction of a water reservoir at Cassis has been disrupting the Port-Miou sector, with signed diversions and a mandatory detour via Port-Pin on the Col de la Gardiole / Fontasse–Port-Miou approach, adding about an hour. The published end date was June 2026 and has been overrun; current status is unconfirmed. If you planned to descend from the Gardiole side, or a through-route, re-check the FFRandonnée 13 trail bulletin before committing. Also note: no Gorguettes shuttle at all on Marseille-Cassis race day, Sunday 25 October 2026.

And the standing rule of the whole park: no boat can drop you off. Commercial passenger landing is prohibited park-wide, so every boat circuit passes Port-Miou and Port-Pin without stopping. A boat trip here is a look, not an arrival.

Costs & time budgets — the €0 headline and the €40 asterisk Verified: 30 July 2026

Everything on land here is free: park entry, both calanques, the Petit Prince loop, even the Château de Port-Miou tower on its open days. Port-Miou is arguably the strongest do-it-yourself case in the entire park — no commercial product can put you anywhere on it that your own feet cannot. What you actually spend money on is getting there and, optionally, the water:

Time, door to door: Port-Miou plus the Petit Prince loop, 1–1½ h from central Cassis. Add Port-Pin with a swim: 2½–3½ h. Add En-Vau: 4½–6 h. From Marseille, any version of this is a half-day-plus — realistically 5–7 hours — which is exactly why day-trippers on a fixed window end up on a boat and families based in Cassis end up in the water.

Pack like it's a hike, because it is one: 1–1.5 L of water per person for Port-Miou alone, 2 L continuing to Port-Pin, 2.5–3 L for En-Vau in summer — there is no refill point anywhere. Closed shoes with grip (the polished descent has injured people in flip-flops), hat and high-factor sunscreen (the glare off white limestone is severe), swim kit and a quick-dry towel for Port-Pin, water shoes if you fancy the Plage Bleue slabs, your own rubbish bag, and a lead if you bring the dog. No stove, no cigarettes — all fire and all smoking are banned year-round, on the trail and at both coves.

Summer water rules — the filin, the licences, the red-day split Verified: 30 July 2026

Arriving by water is the one genuinely different way in — a ~45 minute paddle each way from Cassis Grande Mer — and it comes with three rule layers most renters discover too late:

  • The seasonal haul-out ban (1 May–30 September): at Port-Pin and En-Vau you may not drag kayaks or paddleboards up the beach. Craft must be moored to the park-installed cable — the filin — which at Port-Pin runs on the right as you enter the inlet. Honesty note: the park's page words this as an exception to free landing, while operator guidance reads it as "you may step ashore, but do not beach the craft". The wordings differ; the safe operating rule is moor to the filin, never drag ashore. Port-Miou itself is not under this restriction — but anchoring there is forbidden outright (posidonia seagrass); visiting boats use the marina's regulated moorings (day stop 12:00–15:00: €5 for two hours under 9 m, €10 over).
  • The rental licence list: since May 2023, unguided kayak/SUP rental for the En-Vau/Port-Pin sector is legal from exactly three park-authorised operators, all in Cassis — CSLN (Cassis Kayak), Lo Kayak and Corton Beach, authorised 2023–2028. Rent elsewhere and paddle in, and it is you, the customer, who is in breach. Guided trips are exempt from the rental regime entirely — which is the quiet, structural reason a guided paddle is the low-admin choice here.
  • The red-day split: on a red fire day, navigation stays legal but all landing is forbidden — kayaks are not exempt, whatever you have read. You can be legally afloat 20 m from a beach you are legally forbidden to touch.

Booking it, honestly

If you want the water without the licence-checking homework, the Cassis sea-kayak tour (★4.6, 78 reviews, ~€80) is the straightforward guided answer — guided means the three-operator rental rule simply does not apply to you, and it is the only format that legally puts you in the water at Port-Pin and En-Vau in one outing. The half-day kayak + hike combo covers both halves of this page in one small-group half day — rated ★4.9 but from only 27 reviews, so treat the score as promising rather than proven; the flip side of small groups is that dates genuinely sell out. Swimming, honestly

Port-Miou: no

A marina is not a lido

Clear emerald water, yes — and fuel film, mooring lines, dinghy traffic and hull swing in a channel as narrow as 40 m. In-channel swimming is unsafe and antisocial, and the head-of-calanque gravel patch is a boat strip. The local workaround: the smooth limestone slabs of the Plage Bleue on the seaward side of the presqu'île — deep rock entry, no ladder, water shoes strongly advised. Keep children off the pontoons; they are working infrastructure and off-limits. More alternatives on the swimming page.

Port-Pin: yes

The walk-in calanque

A shelving beach of fine gravel and coarse sand you can simply walk into — children can stand on the shelf before it drops away. The inlet is sheltered and usually flat calm (0.1–0.3 m in settled summer weather); sea temperature runs about 26–27 °C in late July per Copernicus/Météo-France modelling — a model estimate, not a cove measurement. Posidonia beds make a mask and snorkel genuinely worth the weight.

No lifeguards at either — none, ever. No rescue post, no first aid, no supervision; Port-Pin is monitored for water quality, which is not the same thing as being watched for swimmers. There is also no drinking water, no toilet, no bin and no kiosk anywhere past the Presqu'île car park — the guinguette closed decades ago. Carry 1.5–2 L of water per person for a half day (2–3 L in July heat), and carry every scrap of rubbish out.

Onward to En-Vau — the +45 minutes that changes the day

From Port-Pin's saddle, the postcard calanque is one more headland away — and it is where the outing changes category. The continuation is the hard core of the route the park grades difficult (7.8 km / 3 h / +360 m for the full circuit): steep, rocky, hands-on in places, with a punishing re-ascent out of En-Vau in full sun. Budget roughly 45 minutes of genuinely hard trail beyond Port-Pin each way, a 4.5–6 hour day in total from Cassis, 2.5–3 L of water per person in summer, and realistic footwear. It is a superb extension from about age 10 with confident walkers — and a known ambulance-generator for people who attempted it in beach sandals at 2 pm. The full treatment — what the descent involves, where to stand, when it empties — is on the En-Vau page.

The guided version of the trilogy

The classic paid answer here is the Cassis 3-calanques half-day hiking tour with swimming (★4.6, 225 reviews, ~€48): Port-Miou, Port-Pin and En-Vau in one led loop, swim included. Be clear about what you are buying — for Port-Pin alone a guide adds little, because the park has already put the interpretation on enamelled plaques. Where guiding earns its fee is the En-Vau leg: pacing, heat management, route confidence on the scrambly sections, and a plan B when the map goes red (no guide gets an exemption — a red day cancels everyone).
